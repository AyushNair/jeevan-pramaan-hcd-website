Understanding the Problem

The Problem

Why elderly pensioners struggle with India's digital life certificate system — and why it matters.

What is Jeevan Pramaan?

Jeevan Pramaan is India's digital life certificate system, launched by the Government of India to allow pensioners to prove they are alive remotely — without having to physically visit a bank or pension office. It uses Aadhaar-based biometric or face authentication to verify the pensioner's identity.

The system was designed to simplify the annual life certificate process. Previously, pensioners had to present themselves in person at a bank or government office, often travelling long distances and waiting in queues. Jeevan Pramaan aimed to make this process accessible from home — especially important for elderly and mobility-impaired citizens.

In theory, it's a powerful digital inclusion tool. In practice, its error communication leaves many elderly users stranded.

Current Pain Points

Generic "Verification Failed"

When photo-based face verification fails, the system displays only a generic "Verification Failed — Please try again" message. There is no explanation of what went wrong — whether it was a lighting issue, face positioning, camera quality, or something else entirely.

No Guidance on What to Fix

Elderly users have no way to diagnose the problem. Was the room too dark? Was their face partially covered? Was the photo blurry? Without specific feedback, they are left guessing — and usually guessing wrong.

Anxiety About Missing Deadlines

Pension payments depend on submitting the life certificate before the deadline. Repeated failed attempts without understanding why create significant anxiety. Many elderly pensioners worry they will lose their pension — their primary or only source of income.

Forced Physical Visits

After multiple failed attempts, many pensioners give up on the digital process entirely. They revert to physically visiting pension offices — often involving long travel, waiting in queues, and needing assistance. This completely defeats the purpose of Jeevan Pramaan.

The experience in a nutshell: An 82-year-old pensioner takes a photo. The system says "Verification Failed." He retries the same photo. Failed again. He asks his grandson for help. Neither of them knows what's wrong. Eventually, they take a bus to the pension office — a trip that costs half a day and significant physical effort.

The Bigger Picture: India's Digital Divide

India's elderly population is growing rapidly, and government services are increasingly going digital. But digital literacy among older citizens remains low, creating a widening gap between the services designed for them and their ability to use those services.

For many of these pensioners, the Jeevan Pramaan system is their only interaction with digital government services. If this experience is confusing and frustrating, it reinforces the perception that technology is "not for them" — pushing them further away from digital inclusion rather than bringing them in.
